Time-to-detect

Decoding is a long-tail game

Median first-detect was 39 seconds - three full 13-second blink cycles past the unavoidable signal warmup. Only 5 of 44 phones decoded inside one cycle past warmup; the rest took two, three, sometimes twelve cycles before the variance gate let them through.

Over time

A shallow ramp, then a long chase

The cumulative detection curve flatlines for the first 13 seconds (the cycle floor - nothing can decode before then), then climbs steeply for a minute as the bright, well-positioned phones land, then turns into a slow accumulation across the remaining three minutes. The faint blue area underneath is live grid points above the variance gate - about 0.75% of the 32,400-point sampling grid was rhythmic enough to be a candidate at any moment.

Spatial

Where in the room you were

Each detected phone plotted in its actual camera-frame position, colour-graded green (fast) to orange (slow). What jumps out: the slowest-to-decode aren't randomly distributed - they cluster toward the edges of the frame, which is consistent with narrower viewing angle and lower amplitude reaching the sensor.

Confidence

Most decodes were strong. Two were borderline.

Nine phones decoded at perfect 1.000 confidence - every bit recovered cleanly, no error correction needed. Two were borderline: #19 at 0.458 and #36 at 0.561. Both still landed inside the phantom-suppression radius, so the validator kept them. The system also rejected three false decodes mid-run (IDs 39, 47, 216) - phantoms from sensor noise that the valid-ID check filtered before they ever became "detections".

A number that surprised me

"94% detected" is only 80% of who originally clicked

At one point during the run, the HUD said 55 phones connected. By the end it was 47. That's not a bug - that's eight people who opened the URL, got allocated an ID, and then closed the tab for long enough that the server's 90-second heartbeat reaper removed them from the active set.

For a room full of testers, this is the most interesting kind of number: my detector SLO (a signal-processing problem) and my audience-engagement SLO (a UX problem) are different things, and they need different alarms. Both are honest. Both matter. Neither tells the whole story alone.

The brightness story

"Slider at max" doesn't mean "panel at max"

Someone in the audience told me afterwards that their Android phone looked dim during the blink phase even with the brightness slider all the way up.

That's almost certainly ABL - Automatic Brightness Limiting on the OLED panel. Modern Androids throttle the panel when it's asked to display sustained solid white - burn-in and thermal protection. Solid white at 300 ms per phase is the worst-case input. The brightness slider is a request; the panel firmware vetoes it. Adaptive Brightness, Battery Saver, and Always-On Display add their own caps on top, and at least one is on by default on every modern Android.

Probably explains part of the long tail, and quite possibly two of the three misses.
